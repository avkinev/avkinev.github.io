## Introduction

Once, I learned that a bodhisattva is a person, a disciple of the Buddha, who has attained the highest level of enlightenment—almost as high as the Teacher himself. I read that one such man, or so they say, came from India to southern China and brought the Chinese the knowledge of the Middle Way. He was supposedly the one who started what is called Chan Buddhism, which later made its way to Japan and became Zen Buddhism. And, of course, everybody knows everything about Zen.

It seems to me that the path to enlightenment must have taken up a considerable part of the bodhisattva’s life. So he must have been rather elderly, if not downright old, when he traveled to China. Which means, in turn, that he could not have walked very fast. He must have stopped to rest and recover his strength. He must have met all kinds of strangers and heard all kinds of stories, seen and learned many interesting things. Wouldn’t you think?

That is what I intend to tell those who may be interested. Here are the entirely true stories the bodhisattva heard on his journey from west to east.

## 1. The Bodhisattva and the Old Man

So, the bodhisattva was traveling to the east. He walked, making his way through dense forests teeming with wild predators, across rushing mountain rivers, and through valleys strewn with enormous boulders. One day, in the heat, he crossed a rocky plateau and came upon an old man sitting in the shade of a tree. The bodhisattva approached and greeted him. The old man greeted him in return and invited to sit in the shade and rest. The bodhisattva sat opposite the old man, and they began to talk. And they talked about all sorts of things, effortlessly understanding each other. Whatever subject would come up, they immediately found common ground. You know how it is when you talk to your best friend: he understands you at once, and you understand him just as well.

At one point the old man said to the bodhisattva, “How about I tell you how the human soul is put together?” The bodhisattva said with a keen interest: “Of course, tell me. I would like to hear that.” And the old man began to explain how the human soul is made, how it works, how it feels. Why sometimes a soul longs to soar up and wide as though on widespread wings, while at other times it hides like a small mouse somewhere deep inside, pining in loneliness and fear. And so perfectly he explained everything that the bodhisattva was spellbound. “The old man really knows all about it,” he thought. “And is laying it all out so well - just like a bodhisattva!”

“But I am the bodhisattva,” he thought – and felt instantly ashamed.

<h2 id="story-2">2. The Bodhisattva and the Princess</h2>

So, the bodhisattva was traveling to the east. One day, he was making his way through a dense forest and, when it had already grown quite dark, suddenly emerged into a large clearing, with a magnificent castle overshadowing it. The bodhisattva came to the gates and found them open. He entered the courtyard, went up the steps – the door was unlocked. There was nobody at the entrance, inside or out. So, he went in and found himself in a spacious hall with a tall throne in the middle. At the foot of the throne, he noticed some strange creature. He came closer and saw what appeared to be a man, only terribly overgrown with hair and filthy, curled up on the floor, sort of moaning and complaining. The bodhisattva asked him who he was and what was wrong with him. In reply he heard only whimpering and something like, “Yes-yes, no-no.”

Then a door opened in the side wall, and two women entered the hall. One was obviously a lady of high standing – a beautiful woman, richly dressed. The other was dressed more simply, apparently a maidservant. They exchanged greetings. The beautiful woman said to the bodhisattva, “You must be tired from your journey, good sir. Allow me to offer you some tea.”

The women led their guest into a small sitting room. The maidservant went to prepare the tea, and the guest and his hostess began to talk. The bodhisattva told her a little about himself and, of course, asked who that poor, humiliated man at the foot of the throne was, and for that matter, what sort of place this was, who she was, where all the people had gone, and so on.

“That man is my husband. I understand that all this looks very strange,” said the beautiful woman. “Indeed, no one lives in the castle except the three of us. I can see that you are a man of great experience, so I will tell you everything that happened to us. Do drink your tea, help yourself.” (By then the maidservant had already set a pot of green tea and a modest supper on the table.)

And the beautiful woman told him her story.

“I am the daughter of the king of a northern country. I was given in marriage to a prince – the son of the ruler of this southern kingdom. At the insistence of his father, the king, the prince had traveled across the ocean to study the sciences, and when he returned home, we were betrothed. The prince was very handsome – tall and slender, with thick black hair and pearly skin. No one could match him in combat or in games requiring a sharp mind. But most important of all, he was a wonderful person – honest and decent. He had a kind soul and always tried to help other people, those whom fate had not blessed with such intelligence and wealth.

“Our marriage, as happens, had been pre-arranged by our parents. But when I came to know the prince better, I loved him with all my heart – not for his father’s riches, not for his high position, not even for his beauty or intelligence, but for the kindness of his immortal soul.

“We lived here in the castle with the king and all his entourage. And I must say, there were always a great many people in the castle. All kinds of nobles crowded around all day long, helping the king govern his country and his people. These nobles were not much like us, people of royal blood. They were short, with large bellies and blemished skin. To tell the truth, they were not very pleasant people – they often smelled of sweat and garlic. And although they spoke our language, they pronounced words so differently that it was hard to understand them.

“When the king died, my husband took his throne and began spending a great deal of time with the nobles, learning how to govern the country. It distressed him that they did not understand him. The prince had many different ideas about how to help his people live better lives. After all, my prince had studied at the best universities abroad, and was an all-around smart man. And thus he decided that he himself had to learn to understand the nobles – to speak like them, dress like them, party like them – in short, to try to become like them. And since he was persistent, he succeeded. Now he was spending his days among the nobles: talking with them, dressing like them, partying like them, laughing like them. He had a kind soul and a great deal of wealth, so he was always helping them and their families, giving presents to their wives and children, refusing no one anything. In the end, he became just like them.

“But that was not enough for him. Beyond the high wall of the castle lived the ordinary people – those who worked in the fields growing crops or digging peat in the bogs, of which there were a great many around our castle. These people were nothing like us, people of royal blood, or even the nobles. The commoners were small in stature, dressed in rags, with dirty hair that was never combed. From the peat dust that had become forever ingrained in their skin, they were dark brown, almost black. But most important, these people were almost impossible to understand. They spoke some dialect that even the nobles could not make out. And so my husband went among these people and began learning to speak their language. He wanted to understand them, to know what troubled them, so he could help them and make their lives better.

“This was very difficult, because it was not only a matter of language but of a whole other way of life. My husband spent days and nights with the ordinary people. He came home late, sometimes staying away for days at a time. He would come home and fall into bed, exhausted – without undressing, without even taking off his boots. A long time passed, and eventually he learned to speak with the ordinary people and understand them. He not only gave them money – he taught them how to live properly and how to work efficiently. My husband came to understand the problems of the ordinary people. He took part in their lives and celebrations, sat at the same table with them, went out into the fields with them. And he became completely indistinguishable from them.

“And that was when the nobles left the castle. They said to my husband, ‘You have changed. Once you were a being of a higher order, the son of a king. And while we did not understand you at all, we had no choice but to accept you as you were. Then you became one of us, and we welcomed you into our circle and listened to you as an equal. But now you have turned into a commoner, you are no longer our equal, and we will not serve you.’ And they left, and all the servants followed them. Only my faithful maidservant, who had come from the north with me, remained.

“And some time later, the commoners drove my husband away. They said to him, ‘Once you were a prince, the son of a king. While we did not understand you, we had to listen to you because, to us, you were a higher being, the son of the king. Then you became like all the nobles, and we went on listening, still not really understanding you, but respecting your position. But now you have become one of us. You are just like us, so what can you teach us? What do you know about our lives that we do not know ourselves? And although you now look like us, you are not one of us. You are an outsider. Go away, or we will stone you.’

“And my husband returned to the castle. When the maidservant and I tried to take off his worn, dirty clothes, it turned out they had grown into his body. The skin of his face had changed – it had become wrinkled and turned brown. His hair had grown dull and so matted that it could no longer be combed. He seemed to have grown shorter and become stooped. And now he curls at the foot of the throne all day long, moaning repeatedly, ‘Yes-yes, no-no,’ but nothing else.”

The bodhisattva asked, “Tell me, beautiful lady, why have you not left this man? Since he has changed so much, you could easily have gone away. You have family in the north, and they would obviously have helped you.”

The woman was silent for a moment and then replied, “You see, good sir, I did not fall in love with my husband for his beauty – I myself am beautiful. Nor because he is of noble birth – I myself am a king’s daughter. And certainly not for his riches – my family has more money than we could ever count. I fell in love with my prince for the boundless kindness of his immortal soul. Although he has changed on the outside, his soul has not changed at all, and I will never leave him.”

And thus the night passed in conversation. The bodhisattva rose, thanked his hostess for the food, the shelter, and the story, and said goodbye, wishing her happiness. The maidservant saw him to the door. Just as he was leaving, the bodhisattva asked her too, “And why did you not leave with all the other servants?” She replied, “Good sir, I have known my lady since she was a child. I raised her from her earliest years. Her whole life has passed before my eyes. I love her like a sister, and I will never leave her.”

The bodhisattva stepped out onto the porch just as the edge of the forest was touched by the first rosy rays of dawn.

“It’s remarkable how these immortal souls have chosen such different paths to enlightenment,” he thought, and continued on his way east.


## 3. The Bodhisattva and the Fathers

On his journey from west to east, the bodhisattva reached a broad valley lying between two mountain ridges. He had just crossed a pass and was walking down a gentle path, slowly descending into the valley, when he suddenly came out into an open space. On the side facing the pass, trees crowded closely around this little patch of land; on the other side was a precipice, from which he could clearly see the valley below, the river, and the wooded slope of the mountain on the opposite side. Against the cloudy sky, he could even make out the trees covering the top of the pass. That was where the bodhisattva was headed.

Four men had settled down in the clearing. They looked like ordinary village fellows who had gathered for the obvious purpose of having a drink and a bite to eat.

Even from a distance, he could hear the men arguing loudly about something. The bodhisattva approached and greeted them politely. They invited him to their table, offered him some food, and poured him a glass. The bodhisattva declined the alcohol, thanked them for the food, and began his meal. The villagers asked who he was, where he had come from, and where he was going. After listening to his answers, they had a question of their own.

“You seem to be a wise man who has seen a great deal of the world. So, do settle our argument: which of us is the unhappiest father?” asked a middle-aged man.

The bodhisattva nodded, as if to say, go ahead, tell me.

“I have a son. Some time ago he got married, moved into a house of his own, took a plot of land next to mine, and started running his own household. You see, in our village everyone marries a woman either from our own village or from one that is friendly to us. But he married a woman from an unfriendly village! My wife and I, and all our relatives, tried to talk him out of it, but he wouldn't listen. Now nobody speaks to him or invites him over. But that's only half the trouble. What really upsets me is something else. I've even lost sleep over it. Look. We work our land, and my plot is right next to my son's. Every morning we go out into the fields and plow all day. When I plow, I always turn right. That's how my grandfather plowed, and that's how my father taught me to plow. Why, everyone in our family in the village plows that way. Everyone except my son! There we are, plowing: I turn right, and that scoundrel turns left! I turn right, and he turns left! And I was the one who taught him how to turn properly! But as soon as he was on his own, he started turning left. I've scolded him, and at first, I even beat him, but nothing helps. We can't stop working our plots – after all, it's our land, and we have families to feed. I used to be proud to have a grown son, but now I scold him every day, and it's completely ruined my life. I am an unhappy father!”

They all fell silent for a while, thinking about what they had heard. Then another man, somewhat older, joined the conversation.

“I am a far unhappier father. My neighbor here scolds his son, but at least he sees him every day. My son, on the other hand, went away to a big city when he grew up, and we haven't heard from him for ten years. He stopped writing letters and never comes to visit. He's disappeared completely! But news reaches even our little village, and my wife and I know that he is doing well. He got an education, found a good job, started a family, and had children – our grandchildren. Recently he’s got a promotion. It simply kills me to think that our son has forgotten us and doesn't care at all whether we're still alive or already dead. Surely, I am much unhappier than my neighbor!”

For a time, silence hung in the air. Then the third man spoke.

“Sad as both your situations are, neither can compare with mine. My son was the best thing that ever happened in my life. From very early he had a wonderful character. He was very kind and considerate, clever, an excellent student, and a fine shot with a bow. Whatever he did around the farm, he did well. In short, throughout his childhood I couldn't have been prouder of him. Think of all the things we did together: we went hunting, we worked in the fields. I taught him everything I knew, and he always repaid me in full, treating me with respect and love. Those were the best years of my life! When my son grew up, he decided to become a soldier. And when war broke out, he went off to fight and was killed. Ever since then, I have mourned my dead son every single day. Surely, I am the unhappiest father!”

For a while everyone sat thinking, moved by what they had heard. Then it was the turn of the fourth man, who looked the oldest of them all.

“Of course, each of you has reason to feel unhappy because of your sons. But all of you have sons, or once had them. I have no sons, and it seems I never will! Instead, I have three daughters. Of course I love them very much, but you understand what a torment it is to live in a house with four women, including my wife! From morning till night I listen to women's chatter, carry out their most ridiculous wishes, and look for things nobody needs – in short, I live in a woman's hell. I can't even let out a good swear word, let alone go hunting or sit together with fishing rods. Or simply work together, fixing up an old tractor. All the things that you fellows have, or once had, have never happened to me and never will. Surely, I am the unhappiest of you all.”

The men grunted, each lost in thought. Then they turned their eyes to the bodhisattva, who had listened intently to them all. The bodhisattva considered the matter for a while and then addressed each man in turn.

“I think it all depends on perspective. Look at the alternatives. You, for instance,” he said, turning to the first man, “imagine that instead of taking a plot of land next to yours, your son had gone far away to a big city, become a very important boss, married, had children, and forgotten all about you and your wife. Would you be happier than you are now?”

“Of course not!” exclaimed the villager.

“And you,” the bodhisattva said to the second man, “would you be happier if, instead of becoming a very important boss and living peacefully and comfortably, surrounded by his wife and children, your son had gone off to war and died far from home?”

“Of course not!” exclaimed the second villager.

“Now allow me to speak of your misfortune,” the bodhisattva said, turning to the third man. “Without question, losing a son is an irreparable loss. My condolences to you and your wife. While your son was alive, he brought joy to you both, and the memory of that will always remain with you. Now consider the alternative – if you had never had a son at all, would you have been happier?”

The man nodded in agreement and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

Finally, the bodhisattva turned to the fourth villager.

“Now let us talk about you. Imagine that instead of three daughters you had three sons: you quarrel with one from morning till night, another has gone away to a big city and forgotten you, and the third has died far from home. Would you be happier than you are now?”

“Of course not!” the fourth father agreed.

The men seemed to cheer up. They had another round of drinks and began getting ready to go home. After they left, the bodhisattva sat for a while longer in the clearing, watching the clouds drifting low over the pass on the opposite side of the valley, where the dark green of the forest marked the horizon right at eye level. The bodhisattva would first have to descend into the valley and then climb up to pass across the ridge. The direct route seemed well within his strength.

Descending steady, the bodhisattva reached the bottom of the valley. From here, down below, the mountain ridge no longer looked like an obstacle a man could easily overcome. Now, it towered like an impassable wall, shutting out most of the sky. He had to tilt his head back to see the clouds drifting high in the blue sky.

“Yes, indeed, perspective can change everything,” thought the bodhisattva, and headed downstream along the river, looking for a way to the east.
